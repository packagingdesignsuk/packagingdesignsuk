# Packaging Designs UK

Packaging, branding, print and digital design resources for businesses.

A product box, a label, a menu and a website may seem like separate projects, but customers experience them as parts of the same business. When the visual language and information are consistent, people can recognise the brand and understand its offer more easily. This guide explains how to plan packaging, print and digital design together, what each service contributes and what to prepare before commissioning a project.

## Start with the business goal

Before discussing visual styles, define the problem you want to solve. You may be launching a product range, bringing inconsistent materials together, replacing an outdated menu or giving customers a clearer route to enquire online. A specific goal makes design decisions easier to assess. Instead of asking whether a layout looks good in isolation, ask whether it communicates the right information to the intended audience.

List the materials needed for launch and separate essential items from later improvements. For example, a new product might need a brand identity, packaging artwork and a product page before a broader social campaign. Establishing this sequence avoids developing supporting assets around decisions that are still changing.


## Packaging design: organise the product story

A packaging project begins with the product, its audience and the environment in which it will be sold. A box viewed on a busy shelf has different presentation needs from a parcel opened at home. Before choosing colours or decorative finishes, identify what the customer must understand first: the brand, the product type, the variant and the main reason to consider it. This creates a useful hierarchy for the front panel instead of forcing every message into the same space.

For a range of products, keep the brand position and core layout consistent while giving each variant a clear distinguishing feature. Colour, illustrations and naming can help customers navigate the range, but the system should still work when new products are introduced. Consider the sides, back and opening experience as part of the design rather than treating the front as an isolated poster.

When commissioning packaging design services in the UK, provide the printer's actual dieline, pack dimensions, material information, final copy and available product photography. Ask how the artwork will be prepared for production and who approves the final proof. A digital mockup is useful for discussion, while a physical sample helps reveal issues with folds, small text and the way the design sits on the finished pack.

## Brand identity: build a system that can grow

Brand identity is the set of visual decisions that makes different business materials feel connected. A logo is one component, alongside typography, colours, image treatment, graphic elements and layout principles. The aim is to create a recognisable presentation that can be repeated across a package, a brochure, a website and a social post without redesigning the brand each time.

Start by defining the audience and the intended character of the business. A playful gifting brand and a specialist technical supplier may need very different visual approaches, even when both want to appear professional. Translate broad words such as modern or premium into concrete examples, discussing which features feel appropriate and which should be avoided.

A practical identity handover should explain how the system is used. Discuss colour specifications for print and screens, typeface availability, spacing, image selection and sample applications. Brand identity design is particularly useful before developing a large set of marketing materials, because shared rules make later decisions clearer and reduce inconsistent interpretations.

## Logo design: consider every real application

A useful logo must remain understandable in the situations where customers will encounter it. It may appear on a small label, a website header, a delivery carton or a social profile. A design that looks impressive at a large size can lose its character when reduced, so evaluate it in realistic settings instead of judging only a presentation slide.

Compare horizontal and compact arrangements, light and dark backgrounds, and a single-colour version. Fine details, very thin lines and small lettering deserve special attention. If a symbol and name are used together, clarify whether they may also be used separately and how much space should surround them.

Before agreeing the scope of logo design services, ask which editable and export formats are included, whether font licensing affects future use, and how final files are organised. Keep a clearly named master folder so suppliers do not accidentally use an old draft or a low-resolution image copied from a website.

## Label design: make information easy to find

Labels have to balance brand recognition with a limited amount of physical space. Begin with the container shape, label dimensions and the viewing angle. A curved bottle, a small jar and a flat pouch can make the same artwork behave differently. Important text should be positioned where it is readable on the actual product, rather than only on a flat screen.

Group information by purpose. The product name and variant should be easy to identify, while supporting descriptions and instructions need a logical reading order. Leave enough space around dense information so the label remains understandable. For a family of products, establish repeatable positions for common elements and a clear method for distinguishing sizes or formulations.

For label design services, supply approved wording, barcode assets, measurements and the printer's technical requirements. The business should assign responsibility for reviewing any mandatory product information before production. Test the final design at actual size and ask for a suitable proof, especially when using unusual materials, small type or finishes that may affect contrast.

## Brochure and catalogue design: guide the reader

A brochure should answer the reader's questions in a deliberate sequence. First establish who the material is for and what action should follow: an enquiry, a meeting, a visit to a product page or a request for a quotation. This helps determine the structure and prevents the document from becoming a collection of unrelated photographs and promotional statements.

A catalogue needs an especially consistent information system. Product names, descriptions, specifications, reference numbers and images should follow predictable patterns so readers can compare items. A brochure may allow a more narrative structure, using case examples, service explanations and process diagrams to explain the offer. In both cases, clear headings and concise captions help readers find what matters.

Before brochure and catalogue design begins, organise copy and images by section and identify missing information. Agree page size, approximate length, print requirements and whether a separate digital edition is needed. Review page order, contact details and calls to action as carefully as the cover. An attractive document is more useful when the next step is obvious.

## Menu design: support quick, confident choices

A menu is a working information tool. Customers need to find categories, compare options and understand prices without unnecessary effort. The right layout depends on the setting: a compact takeaway sheet, a restaurant booklet, a counter board and a mobile menu each have different reading distances and space constraints.

Use a clear category structure and a consistent approach to item names, descriptions, additions and prices. Long descriptions can crowd the page, while too many competing highlights make it difficult to recognise important choices. Consider how the design will accommodate seasonal changes and whether staff can update the source content without breaking the layout.

When arranging menu design services, prepare a final item list and a clear price structure before detailed artwork begins. Identify who checks spelling, item availability and any dietary information the business chooses or needs to provide. Review a printed sample in the intended lighting and check the digital version on a phone. Readability should remain a priority alongside the visual style.

## Social media design: create repeatable content formats

Social content works more coherently when different posts share a recognisable visual language. Instead of treating every asset as a separate design, identify recurring content types such as product launches, service explanations, customer questions and announcements. A small set of flexible layouts can support these needs while leaving room for fresh photography and messaging.

Think about how an asset is viewed in a feed. The main idea should be understandable quickly, with readable text and a clear focal point. A graphic intended for a square feed may need a different arrangement for a vertical story, so plan adaptations rather than cropping important information at the last moment.

For social media design services, define the channels, content categories, approximate publishing needs and who will edit future posts. Discuss whether editable templates, export files or both are required. Keep essential brand rules consistent, but avoid using an identical composition for every message. The format should serve the content and make the intended action clear.

## Website design: organise the journey before styling

A service website should help visitors understand the offer, decide whether it fits their needs and find the next step. Begin by listing the main visitor questions and mapping them to a simple page structure. A homepage introduces the business, while individual service pages can explain scope, process and relevant examples in more detail.

Design the layout around meaningful content. Useful service pages describe who the service is for, what information is needed to start and how an enquiry is handled. Use headings that explain the subject and button labels that describe the action. Consider small screens from the beginning, because text, navigation and forms need to remain usable within a narrow layout.

Before commissioning website design services, clarify who supplies copy and images, which forms or integrations are needed, and how updates will be made after launch. Review pages using actual content rather than relying entirely on placeholders. Test navigation, enquiries and contact details before publishing, and make sure someone is responsible for maintaining the site.

## Ecommerce development: connect design with operations

An online shop requires more than an appealing product grid. Product discovery, variant selection, delivery information and the checkout journey all influence whether customers can complete their task. Plan the store around the way products are actually sold, including sizes, colours, bundles or other options that affect the purchasing decision.

Product pages should present consistent names, useful photographs and clear descriptions. Organise categories around customer expectations and decide which filters are helpful for the catalogue. The operational side matters too: the business needs an agreed approach to stock, order notifications, fulfilment and ongoing product updates. These decisions should shape the build from the start.

For ecommerce website development, prepare sample product data early and discuss payment and delivery requirements with the relevant providers. Test realistic scenarios such as different variants, unavailable stock and confirmation messages. Assign responsibility for checking the accuracy of product details and store policies. A coordinated launch includes both the visible storefront and the processes used to manage orders.

## AI video generation: use a clear creative brief

AI-assisted video can be considered for concept exploration, stylised visual sequences and other creative storytelling needs. The useful starting point is a defined message and a planned sequence, rather than generating unrelated clips and trying to connect them afterwards. Decide who the audience is, where the video will be viewed and what viewers should understand at the end.

Build a short storyboard that describes the scenes, pacing, on-screen text and intended call to action. Choose reference materials that communicate the visual direction and confirm that supplied assets can be used for the project. Consistency between scenes can require iteration, so review character appearance, product details and transitions carefully instead of assuming every output will be ready to publish.

When discussing AI video generation services, ask about the intended workflow, revisions, final duration, aspect ratios and any supporting editing. Check captions and audio alongside the visuals, and agree how final assets will be reviewed. For a product-led piece, compare the resulting imagery with the actual product so the video does not communicate inaccurate features.

## How the services fit together: a product launch example

Consider a business introducing a small range of gift products. The initial brief should establish the audience, product differences and planned sales channels. Brand identity decisions can then guide the logo, packaging and label system. The website or shop can use the same typography, colours and image direction, while brochure and social assets adapt the message for different situations.

At each stage, check that the information is consistent. Product names should match across packaging, catalogue pages and online listings. Images should represent the current version of the product. Contact details and calls to action should lead to the intended destination. Keeping a shared source of approved copy and assets makes these checks easier.


## A practical design brief checklist

- **Objective:** explain what is being launched, improved or replaced and how the finished material will be used.
- **Audience:** identify the people the design needs to reach and what they need to understand.
- **Scope:** list the required items, sizes, formats, product variants and channels.
- **Content:** provide approved copy, photographs, specifications and existing brand files.
- **Production:** include printer templates, material choices and supplier requirements where relevant.
- **Schedule:** identify key review dates and any dependencies before launch.
- **Approval:** choose a main decision-maker and combine feedback before sending revisions.
- **Handover:** agree the source files, exports and guidance required for future use.

## Reviewing concepts and giving useful feedback

Evaluate concepts against the brief rather than relying only on personal preference. Identify what works, what remains unclear and where the design may fail in its intended setting. Specific feedback such as the variant name is difficult to find on the small label is easier to act on than a general request to make the design stand out.

Review one consolidated set of comments with clear priorities. Check copy separately from visual choices so a spelling correction does not become mixed up with a change in direction. Before final approval, inspect realistic outputs: an actual-size print, a mobile page, a product listing or a video export. Record the approved version and keep final files separate from drafts.


## Frequently asked questions


### Should branding or packaging come first?

If the business does not yet have a settled visual identity, establish the core identity before finalising packaging. Packaging exploration can still inform the process, especially when the product is the main expression of the brand. The important point is to coordinate the decisions so different suppliers are not working from incompatible assumptions.


### Do I need every service at the same time?

No. Prioritise the materials required for the next business milestone. A service business may begin with identity and a website, while a retail product may need packaging and labels first. Define later applications early enough that the initial design system can accommodate them.


### What should I provide before requesting a quotation?

Provide a short explanation of the objective, a list of deliverables, sizes or formats, available content and any relevant deadlines. For print work, include supplier templates if available. For websites and shops, describe the main pages and functions. Clear inputs make it easier to discuss scope and identify missing information.


### How can the final materials stay consistent?

Maintain a central collection of approved logos, colour specifications, type guidance, images and copy. Use named versions and share the same assets with everyone creating materials. When the brand changes, update the master collection and identify which existing applications also need revision.


### What should I check before approving artwork?

Check the full content, dimensions, variant details, links and contact information. View the design at its intended size and on its intended channel. Confirm production details with the printer or relevant supplier, and ensure the appropriate person has approved the product information.
